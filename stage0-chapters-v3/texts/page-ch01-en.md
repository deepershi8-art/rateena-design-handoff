## Seven O'Clock in Cairo

Imagine Cairo at 7 p.m.

Rush hour. Some people are on their way home, others are just starting the night shift. Car horns, trucks, motorbikes, all piling into one loud mess of sound that seems to get through every wall. Shop speakers fight each other for attention. There's a wedding three streets away. Heavy trucks rumble down the Ring Road nearby.

That's what's happening outside.

Inside, at that exact moment, a singer leans into the microphone, closes her eyes, and whispers.

One word, so quiet that someone at the back of the room might miss it. Only the microphone really catches it.

This whole study comes down to one question:

Will that whisper survive?

Will it reach the microphone clean, against a background of total, black silence?

No electrical hum. No hiss. No stray noise. No air conditioning you can hear breathing. No deep, slow rumble creeping up through the ground every time a truck goes by on the Ring Road.

Will the whisper arrive intact?

And when we play it back in the control room, will the speakers tell us the truth?

Will we hear what someone in headphones in Dubai will hear? Will the same details reach people sitting in a cinema in Paris?

If the answer is yes, even at seven in the evening, in one of Cairo's loudest hours, and whether we're recording a whisper, a drum kit or a row of violins, then we can honestly say we've built a world-class recording studio.

But if the answer is "more or less", then all we've built is an expensive room that looks impressive, where every recording comes with a problem or two.

Section by section, this study tells the story of how we get to yes.
