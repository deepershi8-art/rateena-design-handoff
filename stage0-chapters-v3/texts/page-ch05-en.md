## The Flagship Studio: One Live Room, Three Missions

Imagine a single week at the flagship studio.

On Monday, an Arab pop band comes in to record a single. The song is loud, dense and heavy on bass. The producer's in a hurry and wants it done fast.

On Wednesday, a twelve-piece Middle Eastern ensemble turns up with qanun, oud, nay, violins and percussion. They're recording a piece for an album, and they want to hear a real room breathing around them.

On Friday, a composer and a film director sit in the control room watching a scene on screen, while real musicians in the Live Room play its score live, right then.

That is the real challenge of the flagship studio.

It has to be flexible, and perform well, across three very different kinds of work.
### 1. Modern Pop and Arab Pop

A band playing and recording together, mixing live instruments with programmed parts in one production.
### 2. Orchestral Sections and Ensemble Recording

Recording sections of instruments separately or all at once, while keeping them from bleeding into each other with the right isolation rooms and acoustic techniques.
### 3. Film Scoring

Music played in sync with the picture, so the composer and director can follow the performance and hear the music inside the scene.
### So Why Not Build a Separate Room for Each Type of Music?

Because a room built for only one job can sit empty whenever that kind of work goes quiet.

And because one week in Cairo can easily bring all three kinds of session.

But flexibility doesn't come free.

It takes a generously sized studio, with acoustics and treatment that can go from tight and controlled to open and lively with fairly simple changes in the room.

It also takes lots of audio inputs and headphone points, and clear sightlines between the musicians and the control room, which is where everything gets coordinated.
### Acoustic and Operational Flexibility in the Live Room

The flexibility we want from the Live Room is about more than fitting in different bands and instruments.

It also means changing how the room behaves acoustically, using variable acoustic treatment, movable gobos, and careful placement of musicians and microphones.

That lets us go from a tight, controlled pop recording with very little room sound to an open, spacious string recording full of the room's natural sound, without touching the structure.

Say we're recording a pop band with drums, bass and other instruments. We can use gobos to cut the bleed between players, put the microphones close to each source, and set the variable treatment to keep reflections in check.

But for a string ensemble or a traditional Middle Eastern group, we can move the gobos, pick different microphone spots, and let the good reflections add space and glue to the recording.

It helps to know what each of these tools actually does.

Gobos control nearby reflections and cut down bleed around individual players.

Variable acoustic treatment lets us change how much sound the room absorbs, and its reverberation time (RT60).

Microphone placement changes the balance between direct sound and room sound in the recording, but it doesn't physically change the room.

Isolation booths give more separation for singers or instruments that need to be recorded on their own, or kept apart while everyone plays together.

So we're not trying to turn the Live Room into two completely different rooms.

We're designing a room that sounds excellent from day one, then giving the engineer the tools and the freedom to adapt it for each session.

That's what we mean by Acoustic and Operational Flexibility. It's at the heart of our vision for the world-class flagship studio.
